DARTS CAREER SIM
Master Core Concept & Product Vision
Version 1.1 — Revised Hybrid-First Direction
1. PROJECT OVERVIEW
Darts Career Sim is a long-term single-player professional darts career simulation in which the player creates their own darts player and attempts to progress from an unknown local/pub-level player to the highest level of the professional game.
The game combines:
Darts match simulation
Career progression
Player development
Training
Fatigue
Confidence
Injuries
Ageing
Equipment
Finances
Lifestyle
Tournament progression
Rankings
Rivalries
AI-controlled players
Real-world-inspired darts structures
Manual real-dart hybrid play
Future automatic camera-based dart detection
The primary design philosophy is:
The game should simulate a believable darts career rather than simply simulate darts matches.
The player should feel that they are managing the career of an actual darts player whose ability, form, decisions, finances, equipment, health, age, opponents and circumstances all influence their journey.
2. LONG-TERM VISION
The ultimate version of Darts Career Sim should allow two fundamentally different ways of playing.
2.1 Simulation Mode
The player can play their career entirely inside the game.
The game statistically resolves their darts performance.
For example:
Player:
Scoring ability
Doubling ability
Consistency
Pressure ability
Stamina
Confidence
Fatigue
Equipment
Current form
are combined with controlled randomness and the opponent's equivalent attributes.
The game then determines the outcome of visits, legs and matches.
The player can therefore progress through an entire career without physically throwing darts.
3. HYBRID MODE
Hybrid Mode is one of the most important long-term features of the project.
It allows the player to physically play darts in the real world while the game handles the career, opponent and match simulation.
3.1 V1 HYBRID — MANUAL SCORE INPUT
Hybrid Mode should be included in Version 1 using manual score entry.
The player physically throws darts at a normal real-life dartboard.
After each visit, the player enters the result into the game.
Example:
The game displays:
YOUR TURN
501 remaining.
The player throws:
Dart 1: T20
Dart 2: T20
Dart 3: 20
The player enters:
140
The game updates the player's remaining score to:
361
The CPU then takes its turn.
The CPU's visit is generated by the simulation engine according to its attributes, form, confidence, fatigue, pressure and other relevant modifiers.
The game continues until the leg is completed.
This allows the entire hybrid match system to be developed and tested before computer vision exists.
4. V2 AUTOMATIC CAMERA HYBRID
The ultimate hybrid system will allow the player to physically throw darts while the device automatically detects the results.
The phone camera should observe the dartboard.
After the player throws their three darts, computer vision attempts to determine:
Dart position
Board segment
Number
Single/double/triple
Bull/bullseye
Whether a dart is actually present
Potentially dart removal between visits
The system calculates the resulting score.
However, the camera should not automatically change the player's score without confirmation.
The player should be shown something similar to:
CAMERA RESULT
Dart 1: T20 — 60Dart 2: T20 — 60Dart 3: 20 — 20
Visit Total: 140
Buttons:
CONFIRM
or
EDIT
If the camera has incorrectly identified a dart, the player can manually correct the result.
This confirmation layer is extremely important because computer vision will not always be perfect.
5. WHY HYBRID MANUAL INPUT IS PART OF V1
Manual hybrid play is deliberately included in V1 because it allows us to test the fundamental gameplay concept without depending on computer vision.
The development sequence should therefore be:
V1
Statistical career mode+Manual hybrid mode
V2
Automatic camera detection
Later versions
Improved computer vision+multiple camera configurations+phone-as-camera support+PC camera support+potential automatic dart removal detection+advanced real-world integration
This prevents the camera system from becoming a technical dependency for the entire game.
6. PLATFORM STRATEGY
The project should be designed for PC and Android rather than Android-only.
This is preferable because the eventual hybrid system benefits significantly from larger screens and greater hardware flexibility on PC.
Android
Android provides:
Built-in camera
Portable play
Easy access to the dartboard
Natural hybrid experience
Potential phone-as-camera functionality
PC
PC provides:
Larger display
Easier career management
Potential webcam support
Easier development/testing
More space for statistics and management screens
Potential future streaming/content creation applications
The architecture should therefore avoid designing the game around Android-specific functionality.
Core simulation systems should be platform-independent.
7. RECOMMENDED TECHNOLOGY DIRECTION
The project should use technologies that are:
Free to use
Open source where practical
Suitable for PC and Android
Maintainable by a beginner working with AI-assisted development
Capable of supporting offline gameplay
Capable of adding online functionality later
Capable of supporting camera/computer-vision functionality later
The project should avoid unnecessary paid services during development.
The target development budget before launch is:
£0 wherever reasonably possible.
Any service that introduces a cost should be clearly identified before it becomes a dependency.
No paid service should be quietly introduced into the architecture.
8. OFFLINE-FIRST DESIGN
The main career game should be capable of running offline.
The player should not require an internet connection simply to:
Start a career
Play matches
Simulate tournaments
Train
Manage finances
Progress through seasons
Save their career
Play manual hybrid mode
This is important for both usability and long-term ownership.
9. ONLINE-READY ARCHITECTURE
Although full online multiplayer is not required for V1, the architecture should be designed so it can be added later.
The project should therefore keep a clear separation between:
Local Game Simulation
The local game can calculate:
Players

Tournaments
Rankings
Career progression
Finances

and:
Future Online Services
A future online system could manage:

The V1 architecture should not require the online system to exist.
10. CAREER START
The player begins as an unknown darts player.
The career creation sequence should be approximately:
Choose player name
Choose gender
Choose nationality
Create player
Determine starting attributes
Choose a limited number of personal strengths
Randomise the remaining starting characteristics within controlled boundaries
Choose initial career/lifestyle circumstances
Begin at local/pub level
The player should not be allowed to create a completely perfect player.
The objective is to create a player with strengths, weaknesses and potential.
11. STARTING PLAYER DESIGN
The player should have some control over their starting player but should not completely control every attribute.
This creates replayability.
For example:
The player might choose:
Strength
Scoring
Finishing
Consistency
Pressure
Stamina
while the game determines other characteristics through controlled randomisation.
This means two players selecting similar starting options can still produce different careers.
12. PLAYER ATTRIBUTE PHILOSOPHY
Core abilities use a 0–100 scale.
General interpretation:
50 = approximately average competitive ability
70+ = strong
85+ = elite
90+ = exceptional/world-class territory
However, the displayed number should not be treated as an exact prediction of match results.
A player with 85 Scoring should not automatically beat a player with 75 Scoring.
Darts contains significant variance.
The simulation must allow:
Upsets
Hot streaks
Poor performances
Pressure failures
Exceptional finishes
Bad days
Momentum
Fatigue effects
Confidence changes
13. CORE PLAYER ATTRIBUTES
The initial core attributes include:
Scoring
Represents ability to consistently accumulate high scores.
Influences:
Average scoring
Treble frequency
Visit quality
Ability to build pressure
Doubling
Represents finishing ability.
Influences:
Double accuracy
Checkout conversion
Pressure finishing
Consistency
Represents how reliably the player performs near their normal ability.
High consistency means fewer extreme deviations.
Low consistency creates a wider performance range.
Pressure
Represents performance under important match situations.
Examples:
Deciding legs
Match darts
Tournament pressure
Playing elite opponents
Closing out matches
Stamina
Represents ability to maintain performance through long matches and demanding schedules.
14. HIDDEN DEVELOPMENT ATTRIBUTES
The game should maintain hidden or partially hidden development characteristics.
Examples:
Potential
Work ethic
Professionalism
Learning rate
Injury susceptibility
Recovery characteristics
Natural consistency
Ageing characteristics
These should help make players behave differently over long careers.
Two players with identical visible abilities should not necessarily develop identically.
15. DYNAMIC ATTRIBUTES
Player performance should change over time.
Important dynamic systems include:
Confidence
Fatigue
Form
Injury effects
Age
Match sharpness
Schedule load
Recovery
Recent results
These modifiers should affect the probability distribution of performance rather than simply applying crude permanent bonuses.
16. PLAYER DIFFERENTIATION
Every player should be capable of feeling different.
The system should not create 200 identical players with different overall ratings.
Players should have different combinations of:
Strengths
Weaknesses
Consistency
Pressure performance
Development potential
Age
Personality characteristics
Training response
Injury history
Equipment preferences
Form patterns
Career decisions
A lower-rated player should occasionally outperform a superior player.
17. EQUIPMENT SYSTEM
Equipment should influence the shape of performance, rather than simply providing flat statistical bonuses.
For example, equipment could potentially affect:
Consistency
Variance
Grouping characteristics
Scoring stability
Finishing stability
Confidence
Fatigue characteristics
The equipment system should therefore avoid simplistic:
Dart A = +5 Scoring
design.
Instead, equipment should potentially alter how a player performs.
This allows equipment to become a meaningful future progression system.
18. CAREER PROGRESSION
The player begins at the bottom of the competitive structure.
Early career progression should move relatively quickly.
The player may participate in:
Local events
Pub competitions
Amateur events
Lower-level tournaments
The exact number of events should be lower than the professional schedule.
This prevents an amateur career from becoming artificially slow.
As the player becomes more successful, the calendar becomes more demanding and detailed.
19. PROFESSIONAL CAREER
Once the player reaches the appropriate professional status, the game should introduce a significantly larger tournament schedule.
The player can eventually participate in major professional events.
The professional career should include real-world-inspired:
Tour events
Major tournaments
Qualifiers
Ranking events
Championship events
International events
Where real-world names or licensing create legal problems, fictional names and organisations should be used while preserving the recognisable structure and purpose.
20. REAL-WORLD DARTS INSPIRATION
The game should aim to feel familiar to real darts fans.
However, the project should avoid assuming that real-world names, logos, player likenesses, tournament branding or other protected intellectual property can simply be copied.
The game should therefore use:
Fictional tournament names
Fictional organisation names
Fictional branding
Fictional lower-level players
while maintaining a structure inspired by professional darts.
The fictionalisation can have a humorous tone.
21. PROFESSIONAL PLAYER REPRESENTATION
High-level professional players may be represented by fictional characters inspired by recognisable archetypes rather than directly reproducing real individuals.
The game can use humorous names and characteristics that communicate the type of player being represented without requiring direct copying of real identities or branding.
The objective is:
recognisable darts culture without depending on official licensing.
22. AI PLAYER WORLD
The game world should contain a large population of AI-controlled players.
The initial target is approximately:
200–300 players
but the architecture should not impose a hard limit.
AI players should have:
Attributes
Potential
Age
Nationality
Career status
Finances
Form
Confidence
Fatigue
Injury history
Equipment
Tournament participation
Statistics
Career history
23. AI CAREER SIMULATION
AI players should not simply exist to provide opponents.
They should have careers.
They can:
Improve
Decline
Become injured
Change form
Win tournaments
Lose tournaments
Earn money
Gain ranking points
Retire
Be replaced by successors
This creates a living darts world.
24. WORLD EVOLUTION
The game world should continue evolving around the player's career.
AI players should progress through their own careers.
However, the game should not waste processing time simulating every match in detail when the player is not involved.
AI tournaments can be resolved efficiently using statistical simulation.
The player's own matches receive full interaction.
This allows a large world to exist without making calendar progression painfully slow.
25. AI TOURNAMENT VISIBILITY
The player should not be forced to watch or manually process tournaments in which they are not involved.
The world can update in the background.
The player should primarily see:
Their own tournaments
Relevant ranking changes
Important rival results
Major career events
News/events that matter to their career
This keeps the career moving at an enjoyable pace.
26. MATCH ENGINE
The match engine must support two input sources.
Statistical Input
The simulation engine generates the player's performance statistically.
Physical/Manual Input
The player physically throws darts and manually enters the resulting scores.
Both input methods must feed into the same underlying match system.
This is a critical architectural rule.
The match engine should not care whether a score came from:
Statistical simulation
Manual entry
Future camera detection
It should simply receive a validated dart/visit result.
27. FUTURE CAMERA INPUT
The future camera system should be another input provider.
Conceptually:
Camera → Computer Vision → Detected Dart Result → Player Confirmation → Match Engine
This means the camera system can be improved or replaced without rewriting the career simulation.
28. 501 MATCH RULES
The primary match format should use standard 501 darts principles.
Players start on:
501
Scores are deducted from the remaining total.
The standard double-out rule should apply where appropriate.
The architecture must allow different formats to be configured.
Examples:
Best of 3 legs
Best of 5 legs
Best of 7 legs
Sets
Different starting scores
Different finishing rules
The specific format should be determined by the tournament configuration.
29. STATISTICAL MATCH SIMULATION
Statistical matches should not simply compare two overall ratings.
Instead, the engine should calculate performance using multiple factors.
Conceptually:
Base Ability
→ Equipment Profile
→ Confidence
→ Fatigue
→ Form
→ Pressure Situation
→ Opponent Strength
→ Random Variance
→ Visit Performance
→ Leg Result
→ Match Result
The exact formulas should be developed and tested separately from the UI.
30. RANDOMNESS
Randomness is essential.
Darts is not deterministic.
The same player should not perform identically every time.
However, randomness must be controlled.
The simulation should create believable distributions rather than arbitrary chaos.
A world-class player should generally outperform an amateur over a large sample.
But the amateur should still occasionally win.
31. CONFIDENCE
Confidence is a dynamic short-term system.
Examples of events that could influence confidence:
Winning matches
Losing heavily
Winning tournaments
Missing important doubles
Beating strong opponents
Losing close matches
Long winning streaks
Long losing streaks
Confidence should influence performance modestly rather than overwhelming the player's underlying ability.
32. FATIGUE
Fatigue should be affected by:
Number of matches
Match length
Tournament schedule
Travel
Training
Recovery
Injuries
Age
Stamina
A player who has played several long matches should potentially perform worse than a fresh opponent.
A rested player should generally have an advantage over an exhausted player.
33. INJURIES
Players can suffer injuries.
Injuries can cause:
Temporary performance reductions
Reduced training capacity
Missed tournaments
Increased fatigue
Long-term consequences
Repeated injuries of the same type may eventually cause permanent attribute loss.
Severe or repeated injuries can therefore change the trajectory of a career.
34. AGEING
Player careers can begin as young as approximately:
16
and may continue toward approximately:
65
However, the simulation must not treat 65 as an automatic career endpoint.
Players may retire earlier or continue later depending on circumstances.
Age should influence:
Development
Recovery
Stamina
Injury risk
Performance
Consistency
Decline
A player should not realistically remain at peak ability indefinitely.
35. CAREER DECLINE
Ageing should occur gradually.
A player should not suddenly become useless on a particular birthday.
Different players should age differently.
Factors should include:
Natural ageing characteristics
Career workload
Injuries
Fitness
Training
Fatigue
Previous performance
Potential
A player with an unusually healthy and well-managed career may remain competitive longer.
However, even a theoretically perfect career should not allow an unrealistically dominant player to remain World Number 1 until age 65.
36. RETIREMENT AND SUCCESSORS
When important AI professionals retire, the world should not simply become permanently smaller.
The system should generate successors.
A successor should have:
New identity
New career history
New development path
Appropriate starting ability
Potential
Nationality
Age
The successor system should prevent the professional world from becoming static.
37. RANKINGS
Rankings are a core part of the game.
The ranking system should closely reproduce the principles and structure of professional darts rankings where legally and technically appropriate, while using fictional branding and tournament identities.
Ranking points should come from tournament performance.
The ranking system must be deterministic and auditable.
The player should be able to see:
Current ranking
Ranking points
Previous ranking
Movement
Rivals
Points available
Points defending where applicable
Qualification implications

A struggling amateur

40. PLAYER AGENCY
The game should combine:
A — Management
The player chooses how to manage their career.
B — Simulation
The game determines realistic consequences.
The player should not be able to control every outcome.
This creates the feeling of managing a real career rather than playing a traditional arcade game.

51. WORLD NUMBER 1

52. REALISM PRIORITY
The most important design principle is:
Realism comes before convenience.
If the simulation produces an inconvenient but believable outcome, believable should normally win.
Examples:
A great player can lose.
A poor player can have an incredible tournament.
An injury can ruin a season.
A player can stagnate.
A talented player can fail to fulfil their potential.
An ageing champion can decline.
A rival can unexpectedly become better.
A career can go badly.
These possibilities make the career meaningful.

54. DEVELOPMENT PHILOSOPHY
The project should be built in small, controlled chunks.
Each chunk should:
Have one clearly defined purpose.
Modify only the systems required.
Avoid unnecessary refactoring.
Preserve existing functionality.
Be tested before the next chunk.
Report exactly what changed.
Report any warnings/errors.
Avoid inventing features that were not requested.
AI development tools should not be allowed to continuously redesign the project.
The Master Specification defines what the game is.
The Technical Blueprint defines how the game is structured.
AI Studio chunks define what is implemented next.

58. ULTIMATE HYBRID VISION
The ultimate hybrid system should make Darts Career Sim feel like a bridge between a traditional darts career game and real-world darts.
The player should be able to stand at their real dartboard, throw real darts, and have those throws become part of their digital career.
The game should effectively become:
A career simulation controlled partly by the player's real-world darts performance.
The player could therefore experience:
"My real darts ability is affecting my virtual career."
This is the feature that gives Darts Career Sim its strongest potential identity.
59. FINAL PRODUCT IDENTITY
Darts Career Sim should ultimately be:
A serious, deep, replayable darts career simulator with a humorous personality and an optional real-world hybrid mode.
The player begins as an unknown local player.
They build their ability.
They earn money.
They buy equipment.
They train.
They make decisions.
They enter tournaments.
They develop rivalries.
They experience success and failure.
They suffer injuries.
They age.
They rise through the rankings.
They eventually attempt to become World Number 1.
And, through Hybrid Mode, they can eventually use their own real dart throws to control the performance of their virtual player.

61. THE CORE PROMISE
The entire project can be reduced to one sentence:
Darts Career Sim is a long-term darts career simulator where the player builds a career from local pub player to potential World Number 1, with realistic simulation of ability, form, fatigue, confidence, injuries, ageing, equipment, money, tournaments and rivals — while ultimately allowing the player to use real darts and a camera to control their virtual match performance.
That sentence should remain the foundation of the project.
